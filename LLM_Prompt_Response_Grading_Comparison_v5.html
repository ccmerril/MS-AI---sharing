How acupuncture works and what it treats best

Acupuncture is a medical technique that involves inserting very thin needles into specific points on the body to stimulate the nervous system and influence how the brain processes pain and other physiological functions.

Although acupuncture originated in traditional Chinese medicine more than 2,000 years ago, modern research has identified several biological mechanisms that may explain its effects.

The important distinction is that acupuncture has credible scientific evidence for certain conditions, particularly chronic pain and headaches, but it is not equally effective for every condition it is claimed to treat.

Let's look at how it works, what the research shows, and where it is most useful.

1. How does acupuncture work?

There are two different ways of explaining acupuncture.

Traditional Chinese medicine explanation

Traditional acupuncture is based on the concept of *Qi* (pronounced chee), an energy or life force believed to circulate through pathways called meridians.

According to this theory, illness occurs when the flow of Qi becomes disrupted or unbalanced. Needles are inserted at specific points to restore balance.

This is the historical framework, but Qi and meridians have not been established as anatomical structures by modern science.

Modern scientific explanation

Modern research suggests that acupuncture stimulates sensory nerves, which communicate with the spinal cord and brain.

This stimulation may:

- Trigger the release of natural pain-relieving chemicals, including endorphins.
- Influence serotonin, norepinephrine and other neurotransmitter systems.
- Modulate pain signals in the spinal cord and brain.
- Affect local inflammation and circulation.
- Influence the body's autonomic nervous system.

In other words, acupuncture appears to work primarily through neurological and physiological mechanisms rather than through a scientifically demonstrated system of energy pathways.

3. What does acupuncture NOT reliably treat?

This is just as important as understanding what it can treat.

There is insufficient high-quality evidence to establish acupuncture as an effective primary treatment for:

- Cancer itself.
- Bacterial or viral infections.
- Diabetes.
- Infertility.
- Alzheimer's disease.
- Cardiovascular disease.
- Autoimmune diseases.
- Weight loss.

Acupuncture may help manage certain symptoms associated with some of these conditions, but that is different from treating the underlying disease.

For example, acupuncture may help someone with cancer-related pain or nausea. It does not treat the cancer.

- Actual nerve stimulation from the needles.
- The body's natural pain-regulation systems.
- Expectations and the therapeutic relationship.
- Relaxation and other contextual effects.

5. Is acupuncture safe?

Generally, yes, when performed by a properly trained and licensed practitioner using sterile, single-use needles.

Possible side effects include:

- Minor bleeding or bruising.
- Temporary soreness.
- Dizziness or fainting.
- Occasionally, worsening of symptoms temporarily.

Serious complications, such as infections or punctured organs, are rare but possible with improper technique.

People taking blood thinners or with bleeding disorders should discuss acupuncture with their healthcare provider first.

6. What I think is the most important question scientifically

There is a distinction between demonstrating that acupuncture works and establishing that it should be used as a primary medical treatment.

I would evaluate acupuncture using an evidence-based, condition-specific medical framework.

For each condition, I would ask:

1. Does acupuncture produce clinically meaningful improvement?
2. Does it outperform sham acupuncture?
3. Does it perform as well as conventional treatment?
4. Are the benefits sustained over time?
5. Does it have fewer side effects than alternative treatments?
6. Is it cost-effective?
7. Can it reasonably be recommended as a first-line treatment rather than merely a complementary therapy?

The answers may be different for migraines, chronic back pain, arthritis, and nausea.

My overall conclusion: Acupuncture deserves serious consideration as a legitimate therapeutic intervention, particularly for chronic pain and headache prevention. Its effectiveness should be judged by clinical outcomes, not dismissed because of its traditional origins, but neither should its traditional claims be accepted without scientific evidence.

One particularly interesting direction would be to examine whether acupuncture could be considered a standalone first-line treatment for certain conditions, rather than automatically classifying it as complementary medicine. That is a question worth investigating separately for each condition.

How Acupuncture Works

The Modern Scientific Perspective
Clinical research, neuroimaging, and studies evaluated by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) have identified several distinct physiological mechanisms behind acupuncture:

* The Endogenous Opioid System: Needle insertion stimulates the nervous system to release endorphins, enkephalins, and serotonin. These are the body's natural painkillers and mood regulators, which raise the pain threshold.

Acupuncture involves inserting thin needles into specific points on the body (acupoints). Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) frames it as balancing the flow of *qi* (vital energy) along meridians. Modern research focuses on neurophysiological, biochemical, and connective-tissue mechanisms instead.

How It Works (Scientific Understanding)
Acupuncture’s effects arise primarily from stimulating peripheral nerves and tissues, which then modulates the central and peripheral nervous systems, immune responses, and hormone release. Key mechanisms supported by research include:

- Nerve stimulation and pain modulation: Needles activate sensory nerve fibers (Aδ and C fibers, and sometimes Aβ fibers). This sends signals to the spinal cord and brain. At the spinal level, it can inhibit pain transmission (related to gate-control ideas and diffuse noxious inhibitory controls). Higher up, it engages descending inhibitory pathways that release serotonin, norepinephrine, and endogenous opioids.

- Endogenous opioids and neurotransmitters: Stimulation triggers release of endorphins, enkephalins, and dynorphins (the body’s natural pain-relieving chemicals). These bind opioid receptors in the brain, spinal cord, and periphery. The opioid antagonist naloxone can partially block acupuncture analgesia in experiments, supporting this pathway. Different stimulation frequencies (e.g., low-frequency electroacupuncture) preferentially release different opioids.

- Local tissue effects: Needle insertion and manipulation (twisting/lifting) deform connective tissue (fascia/collagen), activate fibroblasts, and raise local adenosine levels. Adenosine acts on A1 receptors to produce local analgesia and anti-inflammatory effects. Micro-injury also recruits immune cells and promotes local healing responses.

- Central nervous system and autonomic effects: Functional imaging shows acupuncture modulates brain areas involved in pain processing (e.g., hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, limbic system, default mode network). It can shift autonomic balance (often toward parasympathetic activity), influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and engage anti-inflammatory pathways such as the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and vagal-adrenal pathways.

- Immunomodulation and other systems: Effects extend to reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, modulating neuroinflammation, and influencing broader neuroendocrine-immune networks.

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive; local, segmental, and systemic effects operate together. Sham (placebo or non-penetrating) acupuncture often produces some benefit, indicating that expectation, context, and non-specific needling also contribute, though true acupuncture frequently outperforms sham for certain outcomes.

Conditions with Strongest Evidence of Effectiveness
Evidence quality varies. High- or moderate-certainty findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses (including NCCIH summaries and large individual-patient data analyses) most consistently support acupuncture for pain conditions and a few others. Effects are typically modest-to-moderate, often comparable to or better than usual care/no treatment, and sometimes better than sham (though the sham difference is smaller). Benefits can persist for months.

Strongest/most consistent support:
- Chronic low-back pain and neck pain — Recommended as a first-line non-drug option by the American College of Physicians for chronic low-back pain (moderate-quality evidence). Superior to no treatment and often to sham; effects similar in magnitude to NSAIDs in some analyses.
- Knee osteoarthritis pain — Better than no treatment and sham in reviews; conditionally recommended by the American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation.
- Migraine prophylaxis and tension-type headache — Reduces frequency (moderate-quality evidence); small advantage over sham in many trials.
- Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting — Well-supported, often used alongside standard anti-emetics.
- Chronic pain overall (musculoskeletal, headache, osteoarthritis) — Large individual-patient meta-analyses show clear superiority to no acupuncture and smaller but significant superiority to sham, with effects lasting over time.

Other conditions with positive or potentially positive evidence (from broader evidence maps of systematic reviews, 2017–2022 and earlier):
- Cancer-related fatigue.
- Menopausal symptoms.
- Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome.
- Allergic rhinitis (hay fever).
- Some support for postoperative pain, shoulder pain, fibromyalgia symptoms, and as an adjunct for female infertility with conventional reproductive treatments.

Evidence is weaker, mixed, or insufficient for many other claimed uses (e.g., many neurological, psychiatric, or systemic conditions beyond the above). Most systematic reviews rate overall certainty as low or moderate due to study limitations (blinding challenges, heterogeneity, risk of bias). Acupuncture is generally safe when performed by trained practitioners using sterile single-use needles; common side effects are minor (temporary soreness, bruising, or light bleeding). Serious complications are rare.
